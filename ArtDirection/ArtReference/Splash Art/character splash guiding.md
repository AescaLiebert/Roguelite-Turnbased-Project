# Character Splash Art Guide

Based on visual analysis of the 38 images in this `Art Reference` folder. Created 12 October 2026. Revised after reviewing the Cassandra, Iori v2, and Seong Mi-Na generated results and the user's composition feedback.

## 1. Art direction in one sentence

Compose a character performing a specific action within a meaningful spatial setting: place, ground or support, objects, light, and movement tell one visual story. Use painted color shapes, broad value transitions, and soft ambient occlusion to describe the character. Keep linework sparse, fine, and subordinate to the color rendering.

The defining quality is **coordination and hierarchy**. Design the setting and character together, then add effects that arise from the action. Preserve broad quiet areas, visible distance between planes, and room for the movement to continue. Render with color and soft light first. Treat heavy contouring, carved anatomy, photorealistic skin, and dense surface detail as unwanted defaults.

### Current project requirements

- **Final canvas: landscape 4:3, width:height.** Examples: 1536 × 1152 or 2048 × 1536. Compose for this ratio from the beginning; stretching or padding a square illustration does not repair its composition.
- Establish a coherent spatial setting or scenic vignette with meaningful support and backdrop. A recognizable place may be suggested with a few forms; it does not require a detailed room or landscape everywhere.
- Make the character part of a specific action occurring in that setting. Full body means the anatomy is included, not that the body must fill the frame or stand at its geometric center.
- Plan one dominant motion, a main focal relationship between face and action, and substantial low-priority space before ornament and rendering.
- Treat atmosphere, props, structural masses, and scenery as contributors to personality. Color-matched magical ribbons are insufficient on their own.
- Preserve the user's preference for spatial narrative even when an individual reference uses a mostly abstract composition.

### Why the first generated results missed the target

The earlier guide gave detailed instructions for rendering a character and wrapping supporting effects around it, while leaving the spatial setting optional. The generation prompts reinforced this with phrases such as "isolated character-and-effect assembly" and "abstract graphic martial motion". The Seong Mi-Na prompt explicitly excluded architectural scenery. Instructions against a uniform ring were weaker than the repeated positive requests for crescents, sweeps, foreground arcs, and return curves.

This produced readable figures with decorative enclosures. Correct the positive composition brief first. Adding "less chaotic" or "no aura ring" at the end of the same brief is not a reliable solution.

- Brush settings, numeric ratios, and workflow suggestions are proposed starting points, not measurements or documented instructions from the original artists.
- The named images are 1000 pixels wide. Fine facial marks and narrow outlines have limited resolution; do not infer microscopic texture or precise brush widths from enlarged pixels.
- Many named wish-art references use isolated assemblies, and several are largely abstract. They do not all depict literal locations. - Transparent space around an assembly is not necessarily a black background. Inspect on light, middle-gray, and intended display backgrounds. Transparency is a delivery property; it should not dictate a floating figure with decorative effects as the composition.

### Shared grammar, with meaningful variation

The common language is a polished anime figure with broad designed color shapes, restrained contours, smooth shaded form, and large supporting effects. Use color/value transitions for most form definition; linework and hard shadow edges are selective accents. The compositions vary substantially: upright authority, airborne action, seated elegance, and cheerful riding all appear.

There is no requirement for every character to jump, every backdrop to become a vortex, or every outline to have the same thickness. Use the references as a vocabulary of decisions. For this project, select a reference for its spatial relationships as well as its drawing style: Mualani connects riding to board and wave; Albedo has substantial faceted support below an ordered upper field; Diluc has a braced action and readable broken-ground area; Mona places the figure within a broader atmospheric field. These are different compositional mechanisms, not one reusable aura template.

## 3. Sparse linework and color-defined edges

### Color is the primary edge

The figure should read from its painted color masses, light/shadow changes, and overlaps even when most outlines are removed. Use value and hue boundaries to separate adjacent forms; do not wrap every body part, fabric panel, hair lock, and background shape in a dark contour.

When a line is needed, use a narrow muted color close to the local material: warm brown in hair, soft reddish brown on skin, cool gray-violet on white cloth. Keep it thin, quiet, and selective. Reserve the darkest marks for a few facial accents and the tightest overlaps, rather than running a bold line around the whole face or body.

Use lost edges where values meet gently, especially along hair, clothing, and shadow-side scenery. Keep only the face's essential features and the silhouette's most important turns clearly defined. The material boundary should usually be visible through color separation, not doubled by a line plus a hard shadow.

### Practical edge and line hierarchy

| Edge or line role | Recommended treatment | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main silhouette | Primarily a color/value boundary; add a fine broken local-color contour only where separation is needed. | Keep the character distinct without a sticker-like dark border. |
| Contact and overlap | Soft ambient occlusion first; a tiny firm accent only at the most important contact. | Explain depth and weight without hard outlining every junction. |
| Interior cloth, hair, and armor | Use painted color shapes and gradients; add only a few low-contrast design marks. | Describe broad form without making a network of lines. |
| Face and exposed skin | Soft value transitions, small color shifts, minimal feature marks. | Preserve a readable expression without etched or realistic facial planes. |
| Eyes | A few clear accents on upper lashes, iris, and pupil; keep surrounding contours delicate. | Retain identity while avoiding bold comic-style facial linework. |
| Distant or secondary shapes | Soft edges, low contrast, simple value/color masses; little to no line. | Let them recede. |
| Luminous effects | Define most of the shape with colored values and soft luminous transitions; outline only selectively. | Describe light without tracing every flame edge in neon. |

For a first pass, omit most visible outlines. When a line is essential, keep it thin, muted, and close to the material color; strengthen only a few eye and contact accents. Do not use numeric line-weight targets as a reason to outline every part. When in doubt, remove a line and recover the form with a smooth color transition or soft shadow.

Check the image at display size. If lines read before the form, soften them, lower their contrast, or paint them out. Do not add strand-by-strand hair lines, repeated fabric seam marks, realistic skin texture, or fine muscle etching.

### Line-color decisions

- Skin: muted reddish brown, rose-brown, or a shadow hue appropriate to the lighting; avoid a heavy black jaw border.
- Gold/blonde: warm brown or ochre, deepening in occluded areas.
- White fabric: blue-gray, violet-gray, or warm gray according to adjacent light and material.
- Blue/green/purple materials: a deeper, usually less luminous version of that material's hue.
- Dark materials: use quiet hue/value shifts to separate adjacent forms, with minimal contour.
- Near a strong light: let a soft color transition or light edge partially replace the outline where the form still reads clearly.

**Failure check:** if the whole figure is readable mainly because it has a dark stroke around it, or if surface marks attract as much attention as the expression, build clearer color masses and soften/remove lines.

## 4. Character and background composition

### Design the reading order

First make the **action in its setting** readable as one large composition. Within that arrangement, concentrate recognition at the **face and important gesture/prop**, then let attention discover the quieter setting and supporting details. A character can remain the emotional subject while occupying less space and sharing the composition with an important environmental mass.

The brightest point may be a flame, sparkle, or energy core rather than the face. Attention also depends on recognizable features, edge contrast, isolation, and the directions of surrounding shapes. Keep the brightest accents localized rather than repeated around the whole perimeter.

Yelan's blue framework is very prominent, but her light face, dark hair, raised arm, and white fur form a distinct upper cluster. Mualani's board is large and bright; her open expression remains legible because the face is surrounded by clean hair shapes and nearby dark wave mass.

### Compose the scene before the effects

Write a short visual event: **who is doing what, where, and what changes because of the action?** Choose only the objects and surfaces needed to make that event understandable. A step across wet paving, a shield pushing through a sunlit opening, or a polearm turn above a timber terrace gives the pose a spatial job.

Place the character, major prop, ground/support, and distant mass together as broad value shapes. Avoid fully designing the figure and then filling its surrounding gaps with ribbons.

### Organize spatial planes

1. **Distant backdrop:** a broad sky, subdued architecture, tree canopy, atmospheric field, or other meaningful distant mass. Lower contrast and detail establish recession.
2. **Middle scene:** the ground, ledge, platform, water surface, or structure that situates the action. Its perspective and lighting connect it to the character.
3. **Character and action:** the figure and signature prop occupy specific positions within those planes, with clear contact, overlap, or suspension.
4. **Near anchor:** one restrained foreground edge, object, splash, or action fragment establishes near distance when useful. It need not form a band across the whole image.

Effects belong at their actual spatial positions within this plan. Particles are details, not a separate depth plane. A single foreground crossing does not establish a complete spatial setting by itself.

### Large, medium, and small shapes

Establish one dominant action grouping, one or two broad setting masses, and a limited set of smaller accents. Make some of these shapes quiet enough to recede. Diluc's bird and ground, Albedo's lower faceted support, and Mualani's wave and board have distinct scale and compositional jobs before fine rendering.

Avoid scattering equally sized motifs at equal intervals. Dense detail should sit beside quiet areas. An asymmetrical distribution usually makes motion more convincing than a perfect decorative ring.

### Quiet space must form a region

Tiny transparent holes between complicated edges provide gaps, but they do not create a calm region. Reserve a connected area of sky, soft atmosphere, simple wall, water, or unpainted space. It should read as a large shape with low detail and low edge contrast.

As a thumbnail starting point, reserve roughly **a quarter to a third of the composition** as quiet space. This is a proposed design aid, not a measured reference rule. Keep the face/action cluster crisp, the surrounding scene supportive, and distant regions simpler. Empty space should have a purpose: room to advance, space for a blade to travel, distance beyond a ledge, or atmosphere around the head.

### Protect useful negative space

- Preserve a clear pocket around the face and the direction of its gaze.
- Keep essential fingers, weapon grips, and expressive hands distinguishable.
- Separate bent limbs with a gap, value change, or unmistakable overlap.
- Give the main action room to travel; avoid placing the destination directly against a crop edge.
- Let the outer silhouette have a few clean extensions and quieter recesses.

Avoid tangencies: an effect edge just touching the cheek, a ribbon running exactly along the forearm, or a background spike continuing the nose contour can create false anatomy. Make the overlap decisive or leave a clear gap.

### Design for the final crop

Use **4:3 landscape** as the project's required final ratio. Plan the ground/backdrop, action destination, and quiet region inside that frame before detailing the figure. As an adjustable starting point, let a full-body figure occupy about **55–70% of canvas height**, allowing more room when scenery, a long weapon, or narrative backdrop requires it. Scale is a tool, not a quota; a larger foreground figure can still work if the spatial stage and quiet region survive.

Centering is allowed when the entire composition earns it, but should not be the automatic result of "full body". Shift or angle the character according to support, direction, and scene balance. Keep the complete anatomy readable. Do not require every effect tip or background object to fit inside a closed border: selected scenic masses can extend beyond the frame and imply a world continuing outside it.

For a transparent deliverable, retain the coherent internal scenic vignette and painted atmospheric pockets. Transparency outside it should preserve its spatial story rather than erase the backdrop.

## 5. Perspective and depth

### What creates space here

Many references use moderate body perspective and strong overlap rather than extreme anatomical distortion. Angled props, bent limbs, supporting surfaces, broad background fields, and scale/value differences contribute to space. Wrapping effects are one available cue, not the main substitute for a scene.

Mualani's large board and foreshortened riding arrangement supply a near plane. Chasca's mechanical prop supplies a dominant solid volume. Mona's orbit lines and Yelan's wrapping ribbons describe space around otherwise slender figures.

### Give the scene visible recession

- Establish at least two clearly separated scenic distances as well as the character's position. A near ledge, middle ground, and distant wall or sky is a useful simple arrangement.
- Let a continuous surface or major solid object establish direction and perspective. A pile of unrelated fragments rarely provides the same grounding.
- Reduce distant edge contrast, texture, and saturation selectively; do not paint every layer with the figure's sharpness.
- Use size changes, overlapping masses, converging surface directions, and selective atmosphere to distinguish near from far.
- Tie light to the place: contact shadow on the ground, reflected light on a nearby wall, or a subtle flame reflection on paving is more informative than a glow applied around everything.
- For an airborne action, show an origin, destination, or nearby surface when that helps explain the event. Avoid arbitrary shadows directly beneath a figure with no defined ground.

**Setting test:** hide the magical effects. The character should still occupy an understandable spatial arrangement with a readable action and a meaningful backdrop.

### Construction rules

- Choose a camera attitude before detailing: near eye level, modest low angle, or modest high angle, with a clear reason for it.
- Establish rib cage, pelvis, head, and prop orientation as simple volumes.
- Use shoulder and hip axes to explain a turn; the far side should respond to that turn.
- Shorten limbs that point toward or away from the camera; do not only enlarge hands or shoes while leaving the limb unchanged.
- Make foreground props and effect segments appropriately broader or larger; reduce or simplify distant segments where useful.
- Define the plane of a circular effect with an ellipse. Its visible front and rear portions should agree about that plane.
- Keep grounded architecture, solid props, and contact surfaces spatially coherent.

### Graphic perspective is allowed

Yelan's diamond, Albedo's emblem, and Nefer's triangular structure can function as designed graphic planes. They do not need to behave like a fully modeled room. Distinguish a symbolic frame from a physical object the character touches or rides. For this project's scenic direction, graphic planes supplement the spatial setting; they should not replace every depth-bearing surface and backdrop.

An airborne character does not need an invented ground shadow. A braced stance or seated pose does need understandable support or a clearly fantastical suspension. Avoid painting contact shadows onto empty space.

**Failure check:** if removing glow makes an effect's front/back order ambiguous, repair its shape and overlaps before adding more light.

## 6. Dynamic pose and line of action

### Begin with a verb and an emotional attitude

Examples: **brace with resolve**, **glide with delight**, **command calmly**, **turn with elegance**, **ride with exuberance**, or **float with curiosity**. “Dynamic” alone does not specify a useful pose.

Complete the verb with a spatial relationship: brace on a fractured terrace, glide along a curling wave, turn through an opening, or land beside a ledge. Choose the exact moment so the pose, its surroundings, and the viewer's sense of motion agree. The environment should participate without needing to be as detailed as the face.

Draw one primary gesture through the head, chest, pelvis, and supporting leg or action destination. A C-curve, S-curve, or decisive diagonal is often sufficient. Add a limited secondary rhythm for the arms, prop, or opposite leg.

### Build a believable action

1. Position the center of mass and identify support, propulsion, or suspension.
2. Set the rib cage and pelvis angle; allow a meaningful counter-rotation when the action requires it.
3. Give the arms distinct jobs: act, balance, guide, present, or hold.
4. Separate the legs sufficiently to explain their bend and depth.
5. Turn the head and choose a gaze that supports the attitude.
6. Add hair, loose fabric, cords, and effects as responses to that body state.

Diluc and Sara show that a restrained body can carry strong visual energy. Clorinde and Mualani use more open airborne/riding arrangements. Choose the pose family that communicates the character, rather than maximizing distortion.

### Movement and follow-through

Hair and fabric respond to attachment points, inertia, gravity, and wind. During acceleration, loose material commonly trails behind; during a turn or deceleration, it may swing around or overshoot. Do not send every strand in the travel direction automatically.

Draw large hair locks and garment tails first. Their curves should emerge from believable roots and seams. Add only the interior strands and folds needed to explain those large shapes.

**Silhouette test:** fill the figure and prop as solid shapes. The pose should still suggest its action and personality before facial detail and effects return.

## 7. Background dynamics: personality and movement

### Give every major background shape a job

Use a shape to **frame**, **support**, **extend**, **balance**, **identify**, or **lead attention**. A motif can perform more than one job, but a dense collection of unrelated decorations weakens the whole splash.

For a scenic splash, give the main forms a meaning as well as a compositional job: a floor supports a step, a wall or canopy provides distance and mood, an object participates in the action, and light establishes time or atmosphere. A generic crescent stroke that only fills a gap is an optional accent. It is not enough to establish place.

### Translate personality through the setting

Choose a small number of setting cues that reinforce the intended attitude. A broad austere wall and cool moonlit paving can support isolation and menace; warm stone and open daylight can support confidence; a timber platform, wind, and a receding courtyard can support athletic movement. These are proposed staging choices, not claims about any franchise's canon.

Keep the setting selective. One support surface and one broad distant mass often do more useful work than many unrelated columns, banners, rocks, and symbols. Every added object should clarify either the event, the place, or the character's relationship to it.

### Align without making everything parallel

Plan the primary action and its follow-through, then use the scene to support them:

- **Primary action:** the body's gesture or the path of the main prop.
- **Follow-through:** hair, fabric, and trailing energy that explain momentum.
- **Scene alignment:** a ground direction, ledge, opening, light boundary, or background mass supports the action and provides room for its destination.

A return toward the face may come from value balance, an architectural edge, a quieter silhouette, or a small accent. It does not require another magical curve. Do not build a mandatory closed eye-travel loop around the whole body.

For a forward thrust, a localized pointed effect can extend the thrust while loose material trails behind; preserve open space beyond the point. For rotation, a partial accent may describe the turn without circling the entire figure. For a calm pose, stable scenery and directional light can provide compositional movement without a moving aura.

A counter-direction is useful when it clarifies balance or guides the eye. It becomes a problem when several equally strong flows imply unrelated movements.

### Design a convincing effect path

1. Finish the scene/action block-in without magical effects. Confirm that place, support, distance, and motion already read.
2. Choose one main effect event, such as a hand-held flame, a blade accent, or a splash at contact.
3. Mark its origin and destination; draw a simple directional path.
4. Decide whether it needs an actual foreground/background crossing. Add one only when it clarifies the event or depth.
5. Vary width and contrast according to proximity and intensity; let the effect taper or dissipate away from its source.
6. Preserve the broad quiet region and clear face/hand silhouettes.
7. Add secondary particles only if the main event needs them. Cluster them near its source/path; leave the rest of the scene calm.

Use one dominant motion and, if needed, one subdued secondary response as a starting constraint. Hair and cloth may curve differently because of their attachments, but they should not all become large competing loops. A motion accent can end; it does not need to turn into a border.

The path may be symbolic rather than physically emitted from a limb. In that case, its placement should still serve the pose and visual identity. Do not invent elemental power for a weapon fighter merely because abstract flame ribbons are easy to generate; physical swing accents, wind, dust, and lighting may communicate the event more appropriately.

### Examples to transfer

- **Mualani:** the board supplies a physical diagonal and the wave supplies a curling enclosure; splashes and graphic accents make the motion playful.
- **Ayaka:** the raised fan and upright gesture sit within broad icy rotation; the open interior protects the figure's elegance.
- **Yelan:** the geometric diamond stabilizes the picture while crossing ribbons add space and flow around the poised figure.
- **Sara:** wings enlarge the restrained stance; electrical accents provide activity across a commanding, stable mass.
- **Mizuki:** clouds, creatures, hair, and folded limbs share rounded rhythms; the dark panel provides contrast and structure.

**Swap test:** imagine replacing the effects with those of another reference. If the pose and expression fit every replacement equally well, strengthen the background's relationship to this specific character.

**Scene test:** remove the character temporarily. The remaining major shapes should suggest a coherent spatial arrangement or meaningful setting rather than an empty decorative wreath. Restore the figure and check its interaction with that arrangement.

## 8. Face as the focal anchor

### Observed facial treatment

Face crops from Yelan, Mualani, Ayaka, Nicole, Escoffier, Albedo, Nefer, and Clorinde show a recurring contrast: a few recognizable eye accents and large hair masses surround relatively smooth, simply shaded skin. Nose and mouth descriptions remain economical. Eyebrows, eye opening, gaze, color transitions, and head tilt carry much of the expression.

Mualani's open mouth, lifted cheeks, and wide eyes support her open-limbed pose. Yelan's narrower eye shapes and small smile support a poised impression. Albedo's turned head and restrained features remain compatible with his composed gesture. The expression is part of the action design, not a finishing sticker.

### Facial drawing priorities

1. **Head orientation:** align eye positions, jaw, nose, and mouth to the same facial turn. Account for the far side in a three-quarter view.
2. **Eye silhouette:** shape the upper lid and lash mass deliberately; use the lower lid more sparingly.
3. **Gaze:** keep both eyes aimed coherently at the intended target.
4. **Iris design:** group darker upper iris values and lighter lower color; use limited, consistent catchlights.
5. **Eyebrows:** set their angle and spacing to match the intended emotion.
6. **Nose and mouth:** use the minimum marks and shadow needed at the intended scale.
7. **Hair frame:** make bangs and side locks support the face's turn and leave enough of the expression visible.

Keep eyelashes selective and dark enough to read, while the cheek, nose, jaw, and mouth remain shaped mainly by soft color and light. Do not apply outlines to all facial features or create realistic pores, veins, sharp muscle planes, or etched folds.

### Facial shading and focal separation

- Keep a broad, readable light family across the face.
- Use a soft cast shadow beneath bangs where it helps establish overlap.
- Reserve small darker accents for the upper eyelid, minimal nose indication, and lower lip/chin separation.
- Use smooth value/color transitions for cheek, nose, and forehead volume. Avoid carving every plane with crisp shadow borders.
- Keep saturated reflected light from flattening the eyes or replacing the skin's local color.
- Separate light hair from a light face through soft shadow, hue shift, and broad hair value groups; use a fine colored contour only where those values merge.
- Place a quieter or contrasting background pocket behind the head when effects would otherwise swallow it.

The face need not be the largest or brightest object. It should be one of the clearest and most recognizably organized regions. Facial priority means concentrated recognition and controlled local contrast; it does not require enlarging the whole character until there is no room for scenery. Keep the face and main action as a connected focal relationship within the scene.

**Small-size check:** at roughly 200–300 pixels for the whole splash, the head direction and emotional attitude should still read. If they disappear, simplify nearby effects and strengthen useful local contrast before adding tiny facial detail.

## 9. Shadow, lighting, and rendering

### Desired rendering balance

Lead with painted color and smooth gradients. Keep large light and shadow families readable, but soften their transitions across skin, fabric, hair, and broad effects. Use only a few firm value breaks at overlaps, eyes, and material changes. The character should feel like a clean, polished anime illustration, not a realistic figure outlined and then covered with effects.

Use broad color variation to turn forms, soft gradients to refine volume, and gentle ambient occlusion at overlaps and contact. Retain the reference's stylized anime proportions and graphic clarity; avoid pores, realistic muscle definition, intricate surface marks, hard comic outlines, or shiny CGI materials. Hair is organized into a few large color masses with understated highlights. Cloth folds are painted as broad, soft value changes. Reserve sharper highlights for eyes and a few small accessories.

### Establish a light plan

Choose a main light direction and one restrained effect-light contribution before rendering. Let light and color explain form rather than building each surface with linework. Keep the main illumination consistent while soft ambient fill carries the shadow regions.

Separate:

- **Form shadow:** a smooth color/value shift as the surface turns away from light.
- **Cast shadow:** a soft-to-firm transition where another form blocks light, such as bangs over the forehead.
- **Ambient occlusion:** a soft, localized darkening in close gaps—beneath a collar, between fingers, at cloth overlaps, and where a shoe meets the floor. Keep the boundary diffuse and the value gentle; deepen it only at tight contact.
- **Reflected light:** a weak neighboring color in the shadow, painted as a soft shift rather than a bright outline.
- **Emission:** a luminous color that gently affects the closest forms, with light falloff over distance.

Avoid dark halos around the character, black AO creases, sharp shadow cuts on every muscle, and bright rim light around every contour. AO should suggest closeness and contact, not appear as a uniform dirty border or a 3D render pass.

### Paint in color-first stages

1. Block the scene and character in large local-color shapes; establish the light/dark masses.
2. Turn the head, body, clothing, and large scene forms with smooth gradients and broad color shifts.
3. Add soft ambient occlusion at a small number of close overlaps and contact points.
4. Refine cast shadows with mostly soft transitions, retaining only a few crisp edges where they clarify structure.
5. Paint material differences through hue, saturation, value, and highlight softness; do not draw them all as line patterns.
6. Add only a few thin local-color lines where color alone does not clarify identity or overlap.
7. Paint the effects with luminous color and gentle falloff; keep glow localized.
8. Recheck the expression, composition, and form transitions at small size and in grayscale.

### Material guide

| Material | Color and edge treatment | Lighting/rendering treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Skin | Soft color boundary; minimal contour and interior marks. | Smooth warm/cool gradients, gentle broad form shadow, soft localized AO at actual contact. No pores, veins, or etched anatomy. |
| Hair | A few large overlapping color masses; only essential tips and separations outlined. | Soft lock-to-lock gradients, restrained broad highlights, subtle shadow beneath bangs. Few interior strands. |
| Matte fabric | Folds suggested through broad gradients and just a few shadow shapes. | Soft turns and subtle AO at seams/overlaps; keep most of the surface calm and matte. |
| Smooth dark costume | Adjacent color planes and soft edge changes define the volume. | Restrained broad highlights and color shifts; avoid wet gloss, etched wrinkles, and full black contour. |
| Metal and gold ornament | Small defined color shape, limited clean edge. | A few bright controlled glints over a soft color gradient. Keep shine confined to the actual metal. |
| Fur | Broad grouped light/dark masses with occasional tapered edge marks. | Soft overall volume, very few fine tips or internal strokes. |
| Water and ice | Broad translucent color masses with selected clean edges. | Smooth luminous gradients and a few bright glints; avoid facet lines or sparkles across every surface. |
| Flame and energy | Tapered masses, varied thickness, deliberate rhythm. | Hot/bright core where appropriate, colored body, selective bloom and scatter. Preserve the main directional shape. |

### Color and light hierarchy

Use a dominant palette family for cohesion, then smaller contrasting accents for identity and focus. This does not require exactly three colors: Mualani contains several strong hues, but large blue/cyan masses organize the picture. Nicole uses warm light and hair against cool dark support; Nefer's pale skin and garment areas separate from the green field.

Shift shadow hue according to the material and surrounding light. Blue/violet shifts may suit a cool setting; warm brown or red shifts may suit another. Avoid applying one universal purple shadow to every surface.

Effects should illuminate nearby forms selectively. Cyan water can justify cool edges on nearby costume, and flame can justify warm reflected accents. A broad glow over the entire character usually weakens material differences and facial contrast.

**Lighting check:** temporarily hide glow. The body volume, material separation, main effect shape, and focal hierarchy should remain understandable.

## 10. Color rendering and edge hierarchy

Establish the lighting and smooth color rendering across the entire character and scene first. Prioritize readability at the intended display size, not maximum detail. Fine marks are rare accents rather than a reward for focal regions; keep even the face and hands clean and simply shaded.

| Region | Priority | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entire character and setting | First rendering priority. | Broad local-color fields, smooth gradients, cohesive light and shadow. Keep the overall illustration finished without requiring dense detail. |
| Face and important hands | Recognition priority, with sparse accents. | Clear simple forms, soft value shifts, minimal eye/hand marks; no skin texture or etched anatomy. |
| Signature costume/prop | Identity priority. | Accurate silhouette and color grouping; use a small number of ornaments and clean painted edges. |
| Main torso and limbs | Form priority. | Smooth volume, grouped shadows, soft AO at real overlaps; no carved muscle lines. |
| Trailing cloth/hair | Motion priority. | A few large color masses and soft gradients; little interior linework. |
| Background and secondary motifs | Supporting priority. | Reduced contrast, fewer hard edges, broad atmospheric color shifts. |
| Particles and glints | Lowest priority. | Only a few grouped accents, if the scene needs them. |

Use soft edges as the default for light, shadow, atmosphere, and broad form changes. Keep harder edges for a small number of overlaps, the eye, prop contact, and clear material boundaries. Large quiet planes and smooth gradients should carry as much of the illustration as the small sharp accents.

Avoid uniform blur, uniform sharpness, and uniform micro-detail. Do not spell out the character with dark linework and then render every background surface sharply. Aim for painted color shapes, smooth gradients, soft AO, and sparse deliberate lines.

## 12. Production workflow

### A. Write the brief

Begin with the **4:3 spatial event**: character, action, place, support or suspension, major prop, and intended emotional attitude. Define a main focal relationship, one dominant motion, and a connected quiet region. Choose a small number of references for specific spatial and rendering decisions; avoid averaging every reference together.

### B. Make pose and composition thumbnails

Explore several whole-scene thumbnails, with character, major prop, ground/support, distant mass, and quiet space blocked together in three or four broad value groups. Change the staging between thumbnails, not only the angle of a limb. Test the action in the setting before costume ornaments or magical effects.

### C. Resolve anatomy and perspective

Resolve the ground/support and major scenic planes alongside head, rib cage, pelvis, limbs, and prop. Check support or suspension, grips, front/back ordering, scale recession, and the projected shape of important surfaces. The pose must already belong to the place.

### D. Draw the effect map

Add one motivated effect event to the established action and setting. Mark origin, destination, and follow-through; keep any secondary response subdued. Use scenery and values to guide attention before adding a return curve. Preserve the broad quiet region and distance between planes.

### E. Refine colored lines

Render form with color and smooth gradients first. Add only the few fine, muted local-color lines that improve recognition or clarify an important overlap. Remove contour lines that merely repeat an already clear color boundary.

### F. Block color and light

Establish light across the whole scene: backdrop atmosphere, support surface, character, and main action. Use quieter distant values and concentrated focal contrast. Confirm that place, gesture, and face read together before ornament polish and glow.

### G. Render by material and priority

Finish the expression, key hand/prop relationships, and signature design. Resolve broad body and costume volume, then secondary cloth/hair and background structure. Add selective highlights and reflected light.

Keep the character and background at a consistent painted finish. Concentrate only a few sharp details around recognition points; simplify and soften everything else. Avoid adding realistic anatomy, fabric microfolds, hair strands, skin texture, dense linework, or tiny decorations to signal polish.

### H. Finish effects and review

Add localized glow and particles only where the event needs them. Check the 4:3 composition at thumbnail size, in grayscale, flipped, and with effects hidden. Confirm a continuous quiet region, readable distance, and a specific setting/action relationship. Review silhouette and final delivery background. Fix composition problems with staging, shape, and value changes rather than additional decoration.

### Prompt construction order

Put the positive **scene and action** specification first, followed by **4:3 landscape staging, depth, and quiet space**, then character identity and rendering. Name concrete relationships: "the rear foot braces on the terrace," "the distant wall recedes behind the shoulder," or "the blade travels into the open sky region." These are more actionable than "dynamic background" or "supports personality" alone.

Do not combine a scenic brief with conflicting instructions such as "no architectural scenery," "isolated figure and abstract effects only," or "fill the surrounding area with crescents." For transparency, specify which scenic areas remain painted inside the vignette. Preserve original historical prompt records; use this revised brief for subsequent generations.
